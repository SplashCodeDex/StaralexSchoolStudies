## 1. What this lesson is about

You already count: 1, 2, 3, …  
This lesson is about **how big numbers are built**, how to **read and compare** them, and how to **round** them so they are easy to talk about.

**Words we will unpack first:**

| Word | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Digit** | one symbol from 0 to 9 |
| **Place value** | what a digit is worth because of where it sits |
| **Whole number** | 0, 1, 2, 3, … (no fractions) |
| **Round** | replace a number with a nearby "neater" number |
| **Significant figure** | a digit that actually carries meaning |
| **Decimal place** | a position after the decimal point (tenths, hundredths, …) |

## 2. Place value — the foundation

When you write **4 725**, each digit's job depends on its position:

```
 Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Units
     4     |    7     |  2   |   5
  = 4000   |  = 700   | = 20 | = 5
```

So $4\,725 = 4\,000 + 700 + 20 + 5$.

Going **left** multiplies by 10 each step. Going **right** divides by 10 each step.

```
  100 000 | 10 000 | 1 000 | 100 | 10 | 1 | . | 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.001
```

### The value of one digit (very common exam question)

In **9 463 285**, what is the **value** of the 6?

Count from the right: 5 (units), 8 (tens), 2 (hundreds), 3 (thousands), **6 (ten-thousands)**.

Value $= 6 \times 10\,000 = \mathbf{60\,000}$.

> The digit is 6. Its **value** is 60 000. Do not mix these up.

## 3. Numbers bigger than one billion

We group big numbers in **threes** from the right:

| Group | Name |
| :--- | :--- |
| … billions | 1 000 000 000 |
| … millions | 1 000 000 |
| … thousands | 1 000 |
| … units | 1 |

**Example:** write 3 050 082 170 in groups: `3 | 050 | 082 | 170`  
→ **three billion, fifty million, eighty-two thousand, one hundred and seventy.**

**Example (B7.1.1.1.1 — modelling):** model 2 500 000 using blocks/place-value charts:  
2 millions + 5 hundred-thousands + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0.

## 4. Comparing and ordering big numbers

**Rule:** compare left to right. The first digit where they differ decides.

**Example:** order these: 4 530 082; 4 350 820; 4 530 802.

1. All start with 4 → look at the next digit: 5, 3, 5. The 3 is smallest → **4 350 820 is smallest**.
2. Now compare 4 530 082 and 4 530 802: same until the last three digits: 082 vs 802.  
   $082 < 802$.
3. Order: **4 350 820 < 4 530 082 < 4 530 802**.

## 5. Rounding

### 5.1 Rounding whole numbers (off, up, down)

- **Round off** to the nearest 10/100/1000… uses the usual "5 or more rounds up" rule.
- **Round up** always goes to the next step up (ceiling).
- **Round down** always goes to the step down (floor).

**Example:** round 4 738 to the nearest 100.

- Look at the tens digit: 3 (which is 0–4) → round down to **4 700**.
- If it had been 4 758, tens digit 5 → round up to **4 800**.

### 5.2 Rounding decimals

Round 12.473 to:
- **nearest tenth (1 d.p.)** → look at hundredths (7) → up → **12.5**
- **nearest hundredth (2 d.p.)** → look at thousandths (3) → down → **12.47**
- **nearest thousandth (3 d.p.)** → already exact → **12.473**

### 5.3 Significant figures (B7.1.1.1.5)

Start counting from the **first non-zero digit**.

**Example:** 0.006 845
- 1 s.f. → **0.007** (next digit 8 rounds up)
- 2 s.f. → **0.0068**
- 3 s.f. → **0.00685**
