Section A — Visual Art
Question 1
Drawing a cone with mass shading, and shading two conical objects with stippling and hatching.
1(a) Draw a cone using mass shading [5 marks]
Your cone should be drawn in crayon or colour pencil and fill the space provided.
Shade from dark on one side of the cone to light on the opposite side in a continuous gradation,
with no visible line strokes. This gives the cone a rounded, solid appearance.
Keep the tip lighter or darker consistently with the light source you imagine, and make the edge of
the cone curve slightly to suggest volume.
A sample rendering of a cone that has been solidly mass-shaded is shown in the figure.
1(b) Two different conical objects, stippling in Box A and hatching in Box B [10 marks]
Choose any two objects that are conical in shape. Acceptable objects include: ice cream cone,
funnel, bell, Christmas tree, snail shell, party hat, roof, volcano, umbrella, horn, yam mound, witch
hat.
In Box A, shade your drawn object using the stippling technique — build up the tone with many
small dots placed close together where you want a darker area, and spread the dots apart where you
want a lighter area.
In Box B, shade the second object using the hatching technique — use a series of parallel lines; the
closer the lines, the darker the tone.
Keep the two objects different from each other and make the shaded tones clearly show the form of
each object.

Section A — Visual Art
Question 2
Artworks that educate learners on keeping the school compound and classrooms neat.
2(a) Two artworks you would produce [2 marks]
Any two of: poster, flyer, banner, signpost, magazine.
Mural on a classroom or corridor wall.
Wall hanging placed in a visible school area.
Containers for collecting refuse, for example a litter bin box or a woven basket.
2(b) Appropriate technique for producing each artwork [2 marks]
For posters, flyers, banners, signposts or magazines: printing, lettering, painting or illustration.
For a mural: painting, mosaic or illustration.
For a wall hanging: printing, dyeing, appliqué, embossing or in-laying.
For refuse containers: construction and assembling, or weaving.
2(c) First four design processes in order [4 marks]
1. Empathise — identify the situation or the need your artwork will address.
2. Define — state the problem clearly and write a design brief.
3. Ideate — investigate, research and gather information and possible ideas.
4. Prototype — produce a trial version of the possible solution.
2(d) Two types of balance; explain one [5 marks]
Name any two of: symmetrical (formal) balance, asymmetrical (informal) balance, radial balance,
occult balance.
Sample explanation of symmetrical balance: Symmetrical balance means the visual weight on
one side of a central line matches the visual weight on the other side. The centre line can be
horizontal, vertical or diagonal. Objects on either side do not have to be identical, but each object
on one side should match a similarly weighted object on the other side, producing a mirror-like
effect that feels evenly balanced.
Sample explanation of asymmetrical balance: In asymmetrical balance the elements on either
side of the work are different, yet the overall arrangement still feels even. For example, one large
dark element on one side might be balanced by several smaller lighter elements on the other side.
Sample explanation of radial balance: The elements radiate outwards from a central point,
creating a sense of movement and order. It is often seen in nature, such as a spider's web or the
spokes of a wheel.
Sample explanation of occult balance: A form of asymmetrical balance where the elements are
arranged with no obvious mirror or centre, yet the work still feels settled.

Section A — Visual Art
Question 3
Planning a 20th Anniversary exhibition and tools for assembling artworks.
3(a) Three factors for planning a successful exhibition [9 marks]
Theme and concept — Set a clear theme or message for the exhibition early so that every
decision, from the artworks chosen to the posters, supports the same idea.
Artwork selection — Choose pieces that match the theme and show the skill of the artists,
keeping the overall display balanced in style and quality.
Venue and space — Pick a place that is easy to reach, safe, and has enough wall space, lighting
and ventilation to display the chosen works well.
Schedule and dates — Plan the dates so there is enough time to build, install, promote and clean
up without rushing.
Budget and funding — Work out the expected costs and how they will be met, and allocate the
money so that no area runs short.
Marketing and promotion — Use posters, announcements and social media to inform your
audience and draw them to the exhibition.
Layout and installation — Plan where each piece will hang so that the visitor's eye moves
smoothly from one work to the next.
Lighting and conservation — Provide correct lighting and, where possible, control temperature
and humidity to protect the works.
Catalogue and documentation — Prepare a simple booklet or record of the works, artists and
theme, for visitors and for future reference.
Opening and programming — Plan an opening event and any side activities like artist talks.
Feedback and evaluation — Collect comments from visitors so you can improve future
exhibitions.
Security and closing — Arrange for the safety of the works and plan a proper closing of the event.
3(b)(i) Two tools for cutting wires and papers [2 marks]
Any two of: scissors, pliers, cutters or craft knives, wire strippers, trimmers, small saws.
3(b)(ii) Two tools for making holes in metals and plastics [2 marks]
Any two of: drills, saws, reamers, countersinks, punches, rotary tools, nail and hammer, awls, bodkins.
3(b)(iii) Two tools for joining lead and copper [2 marks]
Any two of: soldering iron, wire brush, pliers and grips, blowtorch, lead knife or cutter, riveting tools
and rivets.

Section B — Music
Question 4
Notation on the treble staff, classifying instruments, reading a time signature, and explaining binary
form.
4(a) Notate ECG, BAD and AGF on the treble staff using minims [6 marks]
A minim (half note) is written as a hollow note head with a stem.
On the treble staff, the lines from bottom to top are E G B D F and the spaces from bottom to top
are F A C E.
Place each letter of the triads on its matching line or space and draw a minim on it, repeating the
treble clef at the start of each staff.
4(b) Classify the instruments [5 marks]
Chordophone: Seperewa.
Idiophone: Mbira.
Membranophone: Donno.
Aerophone: Atenteben and Mmensuon.
4(c) Time signature of the song [2 marks]
Four quavers equal two crotchet beats; one minim equals two crotchet beats; two crotchets equal
two crotchet beats.
Each bar therefore contains two crotchet beats, so the time signature is 2/4 (simple duple).
4(d) Why Yen ara asaase ni is in binary form [2 marks]
Binary form means the piece is built from two contrasting sections, often called A and B.
Yen ara asaase ni has two clear sections with different melodies and words, which is the defining
feature of binary form.

Section B — Music
Question 5
Chord members in tonic solfa, voice parts in a choir, and a Ghanaian composer.
5(a) Members of chords I, IV and V in tonic solfa [3 marks]
Chord I: doh — me — soh (d m s). In C major this is C E G.
Chord IV: fah — lah — doh (f l d). In C major this is F A C.
Chord V: soh — te — ray (s t r). In C major this is G B D.
5(b) Three voice parts at a choral concert [6 marks]
Soprano (Treble): the highest voice part, usually sung by women or young boys. It carries the
main melody high above the other parts.
Alto: the second-highest voice, below soprano. It is sung mostly by women with a lower voice or
by men singing in a high range, and it fills in harmony under the soprano.
Tenor: the second-lowest voice, sung by men with a higher male voice. It sits between alto and
bass and often sings harmony to the melody.
Bass: the lowest voice, sung by men with deep voices. It provides the foundation for the harmony
and rhythm of the choir.
5(c) Short notes on one composer [6 marks]
Ephraim Amu — Hometown: Peki (Avetile), Volta Region. Genre: Ghanaian art music, blending
African and Western classical elements. Example works: Yen Ara Asase Ni, Bonwire Kente, 
Alegbegbe. Contribution: pioneered an African concert-music tradition, used Ghanaian languages
and instruments like the atenteben in his compositions, and trained generations of musicians at
Akropong, Achimota and the University of Ghana.
Agya Koo Nimo — Hometown: Foase, Ashanti Region. Genre: traditional folk and palm-wine
highlife. Example works: Aburokyire Abrabo, Naa Densua, The Destiny of Man. Contribution:
kept palm-wine highlife alive, taught Ghanaian music in schools and on international platforms,
and used his songs to share Akan proverbs and values.
Black Sherif — Hometown: Konongo, Ashanti Region. Genre: a mix of Afrobeats, highlife, trap
and reggae. Example works: First Sermon, Kwaku the Traveller, Oil in my Head. Contribution:
speaks for young Ghanaians through his lyrics, blends local dialects with modern sounds, and
carries Ghanaian music to a global audience.

Section C — Dance Drama
Question 6
Boboobo costumes, Kpanlogo stage arrangement, a performing-art personality, and roles of dance
drama.
6(a) Three costume items for Boboobo [3 marks]
Any three of: handkerchiefs, scarves, head wraps, beaded necklaces, traditional cloth (kaba and slit).
6(b) Stage arrangement for Kpanlogo [3 marks]
Drummers stand or sit at the back of the stage, often in a semi-circle or a straight line, with the
lead (master) drummer in the middle.
Dancers perform in front of the drummers, facing the audience so the audience can enjoy their
facial expressions and footwork. They may occasionally interact with the drummers and singers.
Singers stand behind the drummers or on the side, supporting the performance with voice and
clapping.
6(c) Short notes on one performing-art personality [6 marks]
Martin Owusu — Profession: Ghanaian playwright, drama lecturer and former Director of the
School of Performing Arts, University of Ghana. Contribution: shaped African theatre through
plays that explore politics, culture and social issues, and trained many leading Ghanaian actors.
Example works: The Legend of Aku Sika, The Sudden Return, The Story Ananse Told, The
Adventures of Sasa and Esi.
Francis Nii Yartey — Profession: choreographer, dancer and artistic director; headed the
Department of Dance Studies at the University of Ghana and founded the National Dance
Company of Ghana. Contribution: promoted and preserved traditional Ghanaian dance, developed
contemporary African dance, and staged Ghanaian dance on international platforms. Example
works: opening and closing ceremonies for CAN 2008 (26th Africa Cup of Nations) and the 2009
AFHF Hockey Africa Cup of Nations in Accra.
Ama Ata Aidoo — Profession: Ghanaian author, playwright, poet and former Minister of
Education. Contribution: helped pioneer African feminist literature and drama, and used her work
to highlight the experiences of African women. Example works: The Dilemma of a Ghost, Anowa, 
Changes: A Love Story.
6(d) Three roles of dance drama in Ghanaian culture [3 marks]
It entertains the community during festivals and social events.
It educates the audience about history, values and moral lessons.
It preserves cultural heritage by passing traditions from one generation to the next.
It helps bond the community together through shared performance.
It can serve religious or spiritual purposes at ceremonies.
It communicates messages, news or warnings in a memorable form.

Grouping dances by ethnic group, describing alignment and balance, and the value of storytelling.

7(b) Describe two types of dance movement [4 marks]
Alignment — The correct positioning of the body so that the head, torso, hips and legs are stacked
in a straight line. Good alignment lets the dancer move gracefully and avoids strain on muscles and
joints.
Balance — The dancer's ability to keep the body steady and under control while standing, turning,
leaping or landing. A balanced dancer can stop, change direction or hold a pose without falling.
7(c) Two reasons why storytelling should still be encouraged [4 marks]
It passes knowledge, history and experience from one generation to the next, keeping the culture
alive.
It teaches morals and life lessons in a way children easily remember.
It reflects and reinforces the social values that guide the community.
It promotes unity, because people gather to listen and share together.
It provides entertainment that is cultural and age-appropriate.
It can even support tourism by showcasing Ghanaian tradition to visitors.
