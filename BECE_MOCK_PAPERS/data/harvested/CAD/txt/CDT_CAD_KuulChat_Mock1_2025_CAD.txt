Mark
Solution
There are two major types of movements in dancing. These are:
Locomotor movements (traveling movements): These are movements where the body travels through space from one location to another. Locomotor movements primarily use the feet for support however, the body can travel on other parts such as the hands.
Non-locomotor movements (movements on-the-spot): These are movements that occur in the body parts or the whole body and do not cause the body to travel to another space. However, non-locomotor movements can be combined with locomotor movements such as a walk and arm swing.

Treble Clef Staff
What is a treble clef staff?
A treble clef staff is a kind of musical notation used by instruments that have higher pitches. It consists of five lines and four spaces and is indicated by the presence of a treble clef symbol at the beginning of each line.
How do I read treble clef notes?
Reading treble clef notes requires knowing the pitch that each line and space represents. The lines on the treble clef staff represent the notes E, G, B, D, F from bottom to top. The spaces, also from bottom to top, represent the notes F, A, C, E.
What is a treble clef?
A treble clef is a symbol that appears at the beginning of lines of sheet music. It tells musicians what the notes of the staff represent and is actually a stylized letter G.

Types of shading
Shading Tips
1. Shade in a value/tonal scale from dark to light on a piece of paper
Draw a long rectangle divided into 9 squares. Leave the last square as the white of your paper.
2. Mark the direction of the light source on your paper
The position of the light source determines where the darkest shadows and the brightest highlights appear on the object you’re shading.
Draw an arrow or a small sun where the light source appears in your drawing. The side of the object closest to the light source will be brighter and have highlights, while the furthest side will have darker shadows.
3. Apply light pressure with your pencil to create a base layer of shading
If you're shading with graphite, start with a soft 4B pencil to create a middle shade.
Avoid pressing too hard with the tip of your pencil because it will make your marks more difficult if you want to adjust the values later on.
Leave the brightest areas as the white of the paper.
4. Shade in the object’s core shadow with the darkest value
Draw dotted lines coming from your light source to the edges of the object you're shading. The core shadow is the darkest shade, and it forms a darker band where the light source intersects the edges.
5. Lighten areas closest to the light source with an eraser
Work gently with your eraser to lighten the areas in layers to develop a smooth transition from light to dark. Apply light pressure to transition smoothly from the mid-tone shade to a lighter tone.
6. Add a drop shadow under the object to give the drawing more depth
Continue the dotted lines from your light source past the edges of the object until they intersect the surface below it to find the edges of your drop shadow.

Modelling techniques
1. Coiling
Coiling involves rolling out long, thin ropes of clay and then stacking them to create a form.
The coils can be left visible for a textured effect, or they can be smoothed and blended together to create a seamless surface.
This technique is suitable for building larger, more complex shapes, such as vases or sculptures.
2. Pinching
This technique involves shaping the clay by pinching it between the thumb and fingers.
Pinching is often used to create small, hollow forms like bowls, cups, and pots.
3. Slab building
Slab building involves rolling out flat, even sheets of clay and then cutting and assembling them into the desired shape.
Slabs can be used to create boxes, cylinders, or more complex structures.
4. Sculpting
Sculpting is the process of shaping and carving the clay by hand or using various tools.
This technique allows for a high degree of detail and is commonly used for creating figurines, busts, and other intricate forms.
5. Throwing
Throwing is the process of shaping clay on a potter's wheel. As the wheel spins, the artist uses their hands to manipulate and shape the clay into the desired form.
Throwing is often used to create symmetrical, round objects like bowls, plates, and vases.

Characteristics of line
1. Length
Lines can be short or long, and their length can convey a sense of scale, distance, or prominence within the composition.
2. Width
Lines can vary in thickness, from very thin and delicate to bold and thick. Line width can influence the visual weight of an object or element.
3. Direction
Lines can be horizontal, vertical, diagonal, curved, or zigzag. The direction of a line can convey movement, stability, or energy.
4. Shape
Lines can create shapes and forms within an artwork. Geometric shapes are often composed of straight lines, while organic shapes are formed by curved or irregular lines.
5. Texture
Lines can have varying textures, from smooth and clean to rough and textured. Texture in lines can add depth and tactile qualities to an artwork.
6. Color
Lines can be monochromatic or multicolored. The choice of color can evoke different emotions and moods.
7. Spacing
The spacing between lines, known as line spacing or intervals, can affect the sense of rhythm and pattern in an artwork.
8. Weight and Pressure
In media like drawing, the weight and pressure applied to a line can create variations in line thickness and intensity.
9. Contour
Contour lines are often used to define the edges of objects or figures in a drawing. They can be used to create a sense of volume and form.
10. Gesture
Line quality can convey the artist's gesture or movement. Quick, gestural lines may suggest spontaneity, while precise lines can indicate careful planning.
11. Implied Lines
Some lines are not explicitly drawn but are implied by the arrangement of objects or shapes in the composition. For example, an artist can imply a line by aligning the edges of two objects.
12. Expressiveness/Feeling
Lines can be used to express emotions, energy, or mood. Jagged, chaotic lines may convey tension, while smooth, flowing lines can evoke calmness.

Lines can carry symbolic meaning in art. For example, vertical lines may symbolize strength and stability, while diagonal lines can suggest movement or instability.

3. Idiophones (Instruments that vibrate as a whole)
Definition: Produce sound by the instrument's material vibrating (without strings or membranes).
Examples: Cymbals, triangles, xylophones, maracas, mbira.
4. Membranophones (Instruments with a vibrating membrane)
Definition: Produce sound by a vibrating membrane (like a drumhead)
Examples: Drums, Donno, timpani.
5. Electrophones (Electronic Instruments)
Definition: Produce sound primarily by electrical means or electronically amplify sound.
Examples: Synthesizers, electric guitars, electronic drums.

Facial expression are the movements and positions of facial muscles that conveys an emotion that tells us about the character and the way they react to situations.
A facial expression can convey the character's true feelings. It includes smiling, frowning, eye rolling, making eye contact, scowling and appearing bored or interested.
(ii)
The basic parts of the performance space
1. Stage
The primary area where the performance takes place.
2. Upstage
The area furthest from the audience.
3. Downstage
The area closest to the audience
4. Stage Left/Right
The areas to the performer's left and right when facing the audience.
5. Center Stage
The center of the playing area.
6. Proscenium Arch
The frame around the stage, often seen in traditional theaters.
7. Apron
The part of the stage that extends beyond the proscenium arch, closer to the audience.
8. Wings
The areas on either side of the stage, out of view of the audience, used for storing props, costumes, and for performers to wait before entering.
9. Backstage
The area behind the stage, where performers get ready and where technical equipment is located.
10. Orchestra Pit
A space in front of the stage, often below the floor, where musicians can play.
