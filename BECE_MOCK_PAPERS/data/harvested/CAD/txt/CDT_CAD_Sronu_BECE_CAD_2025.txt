Section A: Visual Art
Question 1
Six-point colour wheel with hatched secondary colours and stippled primary colours; then list the cool
colours.
1(a) Draw and shade a six-point colour wheel [12 marks]
Draw a circle and divide it into six equal sectors, alternating primary and secondary colours around
the wheel.
The primary colours are Red, Yellow and Blue. The secondary colours (each made by mixing two
primaries) are Orange (red + yellow), Green (yellow + blue) and Violet/Purple (blue + red).
Place them in this order around the wheel so that each secondary sits between the two primaries it
is made from: Red → Orange → Yellow → Green → Blue → Violet/Purple.
Shade the three secondary sectors (Orange, Green, Violet/Purple) using hatching: parallel straight
lines; the closer the lines, the darker the area looks.
Shade the three primary sectors (Red, Yellow, Blue) using stippling: many small dots; cluster the
dots more tightly where you want a darker tone.
Label each sector clearly with its colour name and an arrow.
Mark breakdown: drawing the wheel (2), labelling the secondary colours (2), hatching the
secondary colours (3), labelling the primary colours (2), stippling the primary colours (3): total 12
marks.

Question 2
Printing cloth for a political party rally, and weaving a stole for a graduation.
2(a)(i) Most appropriate printing method [2 marks]
Serigraphy (also called screen printing or silk-screen printing).
2(a)(ii) Two reasons for choosing that method [4 marks]
It makes mass production easy: once the screens are ready, many pieces of cloth can be printed
quickly and evenly.
It is cost-effective per piece when producing large quantities like the 85,000 cloths required.
It handles bold and detailed designs well, so the party's logos, colours and motifs come out sharp.
It is efficient: each additional print uses less time and material than other methods such as block
printing.
The colours are vibrant and long-lasting, which is important for cloth that will be worn at a rally.
2(a)(iii) Three wet media for printing the cloth [3 marks]

acrylic paint
printing paste
printing ink

2(b)(i) Two sets of yarns used for the stole [2 marks]

Weft yarns (also called picks or filling).
2(b)(ii) Describe any one of the yarns [2 marks]
Warp / ends: the lengthwise yarns held under tension on the loom; they run from front to back and
form the backbone of the weave.
Weft / picks: the horizontal yarns that are inserted across the warp and interlace over and under
them to build up the fabric.

Question 3
Producing a clay vase and the benefits of art appreciation sessions.
3(a)(i) Two tests for the suitability of clay [2 marks]
plasticity test
fired-shrinkage test
porosity test
dry-shrinkage test
colour test
3(a)(ii) Three steps to prepare the clay [3 marks]
Method 1 (wet preparation):
1. Soak or slake the dry clay in water until it breaks down.
2. Stir the slurry well, then sieve it to remove stones and coarse bits.
3. Let the sieved slip dry to a workable stiffness, then knead and wedge it until it is uniform and
free of air pockets.
Method 2 (dry preparation):
1. Pound the dry clay into a fine powder.
2. Sieve the powder and add water gradually.
3. Mix and knead until you have a smooth, uniform dough.
3(a)(iii) One technique to decorate the vase [1 mark]
burnishing
sgraffito
sprigging
incising
glazing
embossing
painting
spraying
stamping
3(b) Three benefits of displaying works for appreciation [9 marks]
Builds self-confidence: public appreciation validates the learner's effort, makes them feel
recognised, and reduces performance-related anxiety, which encourages them to keep creating.
Fosters creativity: seeing each other's work inspires fresh ideas, encourages experimentation, and
allows learners to build on each other's concepts.

Section B: Music
Question 4
Notating semibreves on the treble and bass staves, classifying Western instruments, and interpreting
King Paluta's Aseda.
4(a) Notate F, C (treble) and C (bass) using semibreves [6 marks]
A semibreve (whole note) is written as an open (hollow) note head with no stem.
Treble staff: the five lines from bottom to top spell E G B D F (mnemonic: "Every Good Boy
Deserves Fruit") and the four spaces spell F A C E.
(i) F on a line in the treble clef is written on the very top line of the staff.
(ii) C on a space in the treble clef is written in the third space from the bottom (the C of F-A-C-E).
Bass staff: the five lines from bottom to top spell G B D F A ("Good Boys Deserve Fruit Always")
and the four spaces spell A C E G ("All Cows Eat Grass").
(iii) C on a space in the bass clef is written in the second space from the bottom (the C of A-C-E-
G).
Begin each staff with the correct clef sign before drawing the semibreve on the required line or
space. 2 marks per correctly placed note.
4(b) Classify the Western instruments [5 marks]
Quick check: woodwinds produce sound by a vibrating reed or an edge of air (clarinet); brass by the
player's buzzing lips into a metal mouthpiece (trombone); percussion by being struck (cymbals); strings
by bowing or plucking taut strings (cello); electronic by generating or amplifying sound electrically
(keyboard/synthesiser).

4(c) Two issues addressed in King Paluta's Aseda [4 marks]
Gratitude and thankfulness to God: the song's title Aseda literally means "thanksgiving"; King
Paluta uses the song to thank God for lifting him out of hardship and to remind listeners to be
grateful for every small breakthrough.
Hard work and perseverance through poverty: he recounts years of struggle, rejection and
financial hardship before success came, encouraging young people not to give up on their dreams.
The value of family support: he acknowledges his mother and loved ones who stood by him,
highlighting the importance of family in an individual's rise.
Humility despite success: he warns that wealth and fame should not change a person's character, a
common message in Ghanaian moral teaching.
Faith and trust in God: the song frames success as God's doing and calls on listeners to put their
trust in God rather than in people alone.

Question 5
Classifying Ghanaian musicians by genre and explaining Italian musical terms.
5(a) Classify the musicians [5 marks]
Art music (Western-style concert music, often choral): James Varrick Armaah leads Harmonious
Chorale and composes large-scale choral works; Newlove Annan is a choral composer and choir
trainer.
Popular music (highlife, hiplife, Afrobeats and related contemporary styles): K. K. Kabobo is a
highlife star; Okyeame Kwame is a celebrated hiplife rapper.
Traditional music: Dela Botri is best known as a master of the atenteben flute and for performing
and teaching Ghanaian traditional music.

5(b) Meaning of the musical terms [10 marks]
(i) Presto: a tempo marking meaning very fast; one of the quickest common tempo markings, faster than
Allegro.
(ii) Allegretto: meaning moderately fast or lightly quick; slightly slower and more relaxed than Allegro.
(iii) Andante: meaning walking pace; a moderate, flowing tempo, neither slow nor fast.
(iv) Mezzo: meaning medium or moderately; used as a qualifier, for example mezzo-forte (moderately
loud) or mezzo-piano (moderately soft).
(v) A cappella: meaning in the chapel style; vocal music sung without any instrumental accompaniment.

Section C: Dance Drama
Question 6
Maame Dokono's contributions, performance-space anatomy, and modern Ghanaian dances as cultural
promoters.
6(a) Three contributions of Grace Omaboe (Maame Dokono) [3 marks]
Promoted Ghanaian storytelling on television: she hosted and starred in By the Fireside, a long-
running children's TV programme that retold Ananse and other folk stories in Akan, keeping oral
tradition alive for new generations.
Built a celebrated acting career: she acted in numerous Ghanaian films and stage plays,
becoming one of the most recognised actresses of her generation and inspiring many younger
performers.
Mentored and trained younger actors: through her productions and workshops she helped
groom new talent in the Ghanaian creative-arts industry.
Produced cultural content in local languages: her work mostly used Akan, which helped
preserve and promote the use of Ghanaian languages in the media.
Engaged in public and charity work: she used her public profile for charitable causes and
community engagement, serving as a role model for women in the arts.
Served in public office: she took on political and public-service roles that extended her cultural
advocacy beyond the screen.

Stage: the main acting area where all performances take place in full view of the audience.
Backstage: the area behind the stage used for scenery storage, set changes and out-of-sight
movement between entrances.
Wings: the narrow areas at each side of the stage where performers wait, hidden from the
audience, before making an entrance.

Auditorium: the space where the audience sits to watch the performance.
Orchestra pit: a lowered area between the stage and audience that houses the musicians

Apron: the part of the stage that projects towards the audience beyond the main curtain; used for

6(c) Two ways modern Ghanaian dances preserve and promote culture [6 marks]
They keep Ghanaian identity visible to young people: dances like azonto, alkayida and shaku
shaku grew from Ghanaian streets, so young dancers who take them up carry Ghanaian rhythm,
body language and humour into their daily lives, strengthening their sense of national identity.
They carry Ghanaian music and language across the world: these dances are usually performed
to Ghanaian songs, often in Twi, Ga or Pidgin; when videos go viral on social media, the songs and
language travel with them, exposing foreign audiences to Ghanaian culture.
They adapt tradition into a modern form: many of the moves borrow from traditional dances
(footwork from kpanlogo, waist movements from adowa), so performing them keeps traditional
patterns alive in a shape that feels modern.
They become a tool for tourism and cultural events: dance groups use them at festivals,
Independence Day parades and shows for visiting tourists, turning culture into a positive economic
activity.
They teach teamwork and community spirit: most modern dances are group dances, so learning
and performing them together builds the kind of community feeling that traditional Ghanaian
dance has always encouraged.

Section C: Dance Drama
Question 7
Defining dance-drama, using body parts in performance, and the elements of contemporary dance.
7(a) Explain dance-drama [3 marks]
Dance-drama is a form of performance that tells a complete story using dance movements
together with the elements of drama: characters, a plot, costumes, music and sometimes dialogue
or mime.
The dancers act out the events of the story through choreographed movement; meaning is carried
by the body rather than mainly by spoken words.
It combines the storytelling power of drama with the emotional and rhythmic power of dance, so
the audience both follows a story and enjoys the movement.
7(b) How actors and dancers use body parts in performance [6 marks]
Feet: establish rhythm and energy. Stamping feet show anger or assertiveness; soft gliding steps
show calmness; running shows urgency.
Arms: extend the body into space and shape emotion. Raised arms show joy or victory; wide open
arms welcome; crossed arms show refusal or protection.
Hands: carry detailed meaning. Pointing directs attention; clenched fists show anger or
determination; open palms show honesty or pleading; waving greets or dismisses.
Legs: create shape and power. Bent knees ground the dancer in traditional Ghanaian style; leaps
show excitement; kneeling shows submission or prayer.
Head: adds focus and attitude. Nodding shows agreement; shaking shows refusal; a tilted head
shows curiosity; a lowered head shows sorrow or respect.
Neck: amplifies the head's meaning. A stretched neck shows pride or alertness; a relaxed neck
shows calm; a turned neck directs gaze and intention.
Torso: is the emotional centre of the body. A lifted chest shows confidence; a collapsed chest
shows defeat; a twisted torso shows tension or conflict; hip sways carry the rhythm in many African
dances.
7(c) Elements of contemporary dance, with examples [6 marks]
(i) Body: The dancer's own body is the main instrument; every part, from fingers to feet, is used to shape
movement. In contemporary dance the whole body is available, so a performer might isolate just the
shoulder in one moment and involve the entire body in a falling roll in the next. Example: a dancer curls
into a tight ball to express grief, then slowly unfolds, using the torso and limbs in sequence, to show
recovery.
(ii) Space: How the dancer uses the area around them. This covers levels (high, medium, low), directions
(forward, backward, diagonal), pathways (straight, curved, zig-zag) and personal space (how close the
dancer is to others). Example: a solo dancer moves from an upstage corner along a curving pathway to a
low level centre stage, shrinking the space to pull the audience's focus inwards.
